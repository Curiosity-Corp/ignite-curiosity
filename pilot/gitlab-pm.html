GitLab as Your Project Management Workspace

In 2026, a product or project manager’s job lives in the tracker: the issue is the unit of work, the board is the conversation, and the milestone is the promise. GitLab gives you all of it without ever opening code. And because every issue is plain, structured text, AI agents can now read your threads, draft your status updates and even open and update issues alongside you. This course makes you fluent in the tool and in working with those agents. Pillar: Tooling (T) in the PILOT Program.

What you’ll be able to do

Turn fuzzy ideas into clear issues

Write issues with a goal, acceptance criteria and a template, so anyone (or any AI agent) can pick one up without a meeting.

Run a workflow with scoped labels

Use labels such as workflow::ready and workflow::in-progress so every issue is always in exactly one stage.

Build boards people actually use

Create boards by label, milestone or assignee, and save the views your team opens every morning.

Plan in milestones and iterations

Group work into milestones, time-box it into two-week iterations, and read the burndown to see whether you are on track.

Read the numbers

Use weights, time tracking, issue analytics and value stream analytics to spot where work gets stuck.

Brief an AI agent

Summarise long threads and draft status updates with GitLab Duo or Claude, then check the result before it goes out.

Practice in GitLab

Do these on your real project board in git.developerdojo.org. No code, and no new projects. Our GitLab runs the Ultimate tier, so every feature below is available to you. Your Project Manager role can plan, label and board but cannot see code, pipelines or releases, so you will not be pushing files.

Exercise 1. Set up workflow:: scoped labels

On your project board, open Manage > Labels and create these labels: workflow::backlog, workflow::ready, workflow::in-progress, workflow::review, workflow::done. Because they share the workflow:: prefix, GitLab lets an issue hold only one of them at a time. Then create a board with one list per label, and set a work-in-progress (WIP) limit of 3 on the workflow::in-progress list so the team notices when too much is open at once.

Check: move one issue across the board and watch the label swap automatically.

Exercise 2. Start a two-week iteration cadence

In your group, open Plan > Iterations and create a cadence that repeats every 2 weeks with a few upcoming iterations scheduled. Pull five issues into the current iteration from the issue sidebar. Hold a 15-minute planning session with your buddy and write the iteration goal in the first issue’s description.

Check: open the iteration report and note how many issues you committed to.

Exercise 3. Create a milestone and read its burndown

Create a milestone with a start and due date two weeks apart (Plan > Milestones). Add weights to the issues (1, 2, 3, 5) and assign them to the milestone using quick actions: /milestone %"Your Milestone" and /weight 3. After a few days open the milestone page and read the burndown chart.

Check: can you explain, in one sentence, whether you are ahead or behind?

Exercise 4. Build a reusable feature-request template

You do not need to push files to get templates. Write your feature-request template (sections: Problem, Who is affected, Desired outcome, Acceptance criteria, Out of scope, Links) in two reusable forms. First, save it as a comment template in your GitLab user preferences, so you can insert it into any issue with one click. Second, paste it into a wiki page called “Feature request template” so the whole team can copy it into a new issue description.

Check: create one real issue from your template and notice how much faster the next reader understands it. If your team later wants it as an official project template, an engineering buddy can add it as a file.

Exercise 5. Quick actions and saved board views

Open any issue and, in a single comment, type: /label ~"workflow::ready", /milestone %"Your Milestone", /weight 2, /estimate 4h, /assign me. Press Comment once and see everything apply. Also add /health_status on_track to an issue you are confident about. Next, make three boards: by label (workflow), by assignee (one list per teammate) and by milestone. Use Switch board to jump between them, and bookmark the one you use daily.

Check: time yourself. How many clicks did the quick actions save?

The agentic angle

Issues, comments and labels are structured text, which is exactly what AI works well with. Agents can summarise a 60-comment thread in seconds, draft your weekly status from a milestone, spot issues with no owner, and, with the right permissions, open and update issues for you. Your job shifts from typing updates to deciding what matters and checking the agent’s work. GitLab’s own AI, GitLab Duo, includes features such as discussion summaries and issue description generation, though Duo may need to be switched on for your account, so ask your coach. If it is not available, copy the thread into Claude and ask the same questions.

Try it: your first agent-drafted status update

  1. Pick the milestone from Exercise 3 and open its issue list. Choose one long issue thread.
  2. Ask GitLab Duo (or paste the thread into Claude, after removing anything private) to: “Summarise this thread in 5 bullets, list open questions, and name the next action and owner.”
  3. Then ask: “Draft a 120-word status update for the team: done, in progress, blocked, and a health rating of on track, needs attention or at risk, with reasons.”
  4. Fact-check every line against the board. Fix what is wrong, post it as a comment, and note what the AI missed.
  5. Bonus: write a short instruction an agent could follow to draft a new issue from your feature template, and list which permissions you would give it and which you would not.

Course FAQ

Is there a free GitLab certification?

Not that we could find. GitLab University's courses and learning paths are free, but we found no free completion badge, and the official certification exams are listed at about $150 USD each. Our $0 path gives you the skills and course completions; the exams are an optional step for later, when sponsored.

Why is the page called GitLab Certified Project Manager?

That is our PILOT track name for the whole journey. GitLab's official credentials are named differently (see the exam steps above), and we only claim what GitLab actually offers.

I cannot see a feature this course mentions. What now?

Our GitLab is Ultimate, so the feature exists. Your custom Project Manager role can plan, label and board but cannot see code, pipelines or releases, and a few menus sit behind those. Note which menu is missing and ask your coach.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. Everything in this course happens in issues, boards, milestones, wikis and analytics. Templates are built as comment templates and wiki pages, so you never push files.

How long will it take?

Plan on about 12 to 16 hours across two to three weeks, including the five exercises.

Track your progress

Practice what you teach. Log each step of this course as a to-do in your GitLab workspace: create one issue per step with the label learning::pilot, and close it when that step is done. Your board becomes your learning record.

Stuck on a board?

Work with a qualified coach in a live session and learn faster.
