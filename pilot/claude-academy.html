Work with Claude like a Great Product Manager

In 2026 a PM who can brief an AI well ships faster and thinks more clearly. Claude can turn a messy meeting into clean issues, draft a PRD, or synthesise ten interviews in minutes. But it only does this well when you give it good context, ask for the right shape of answer, and then check its work with a critical eye. This course is your free, hands-on path to doing exactly that, and then to going one step further: moving from chatting with Claude to delegating work to an agent with a clear task brief.

What you'll be able to do

Prompt with Clarity

Give Claude the goal, the audience, the context and an example, and get useful first drafts instead of generic ones.

Get Structured Output

Ask for tables, user stories with acceptance criteria, or issue-ready text you can paste straight into GitLab.

Review AI Output Critically

Use the 4D habits (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) to spot gaps, invented facts and weak reasoning.

Run PM Workflows

Draft PRDs, synthesise research, and convert meeting notes into prioritised issues, with you as the editor-in-chief.

Delegate to Agents

Write task briefs an agent can act on, and read what the agent did (its plan, its changes, its summary) without needing to code.

Use AI Responsibly

Know what not to paste in, when to disclose AI help, and how MCP connections let Claude reach your tools safely.

Practice in GitLab

Do these on your real project board. No code and no new projects needed. Never paste confidential customer data or secrets into a chat.

Exercise 1: Meeting transcript to issues

Take a messy meeting transcript or notes (a real one with sensitive details removed). Ask Claude to: (a) list decisions, (b) list action items with an owner and due date where stated, and (c) draft each action as an issue with a title, a one-paragraph description and 2 to 4 acceptance criteria, in a format you can copy. Tell it to write "unknown" instead of guessing. Create the issues on your board.

Exercise 2: The AI-assisted label

In your project, create a label named AI-assisted (Manage → Labels). Add it to every issue Claude helped draft. Add a second scoped label such as ai-review::pending and change it to ai-review::done only after you have checked the facts, owners and dates yourself. Make a board list for each so you can see what still needs your review.

Exercise 3: A prompt library in the wiki

Open your project wiki and create a page called Prompt Library. Add 4 prompts that worked for you: meeting notes to issues, PRD first draft, interview synthesis, and "review this issue for gaps". For each, record the goal, the prompt text, what context to attach, what a good answer looks like, and what you had to fix. Update the page every week.

Exercise 4: A review checklist in an issue template

Ask your team lead where issue templates are kept, or propose one as an issue. Add a short "AI-assisted review" checklist to your issue descriptions: facts verified, names and dates checked, nothing confidential included, acceptance criteria testable. Tick it before you move the issue out of Open. After two weeks, filter by the AI-assisted label and note how many issues needed edits.

The agentic angle: chatting vs. delegating

When you chat, you and Claude go back and forth and you check every answer as it arrives. When you delegate to an agent, it takes many steps by itself (reading, planning, changing things) and you check the result at the end. That means the quality of your task brief matters far more, because you are not there to steer each step. In the AI Fluency language, this is Delegation (what to hand over) and Description (how clearly you say it), followed by Discernment and Diligence when the work comes back.

A good task brief has six parts

Goal (the outcome, and why) · Context (links to the issue, the doc, the people) · Scope (what is in and, just as important, out) · Constraints (do not touch X, ask before Y) · Definition of done (testable checks) · Report-back (summarise what you did, what you were unsure about, and what you did not do).

Practical exercise: brief, read, review

Pick one small, low-risk issue (for example "tidy the labels on the board" or "summarise last sprint's closed issues"). Write a six-part task brief as an issue comment. If your team has an agent set up, hand it over. Otherwise, ask Claude to act as the agent and reply with the plan it would follow. Then read the plan or the agent's summary critically: Did it stay in scope? Did it say what it was unsure about? What would you add to the brief next time? Save your best brief to the wiki Prompt Library as a template.

Claude Academy FAQ

Do I need to pay for anything?

No. Each course shows a free registration button. Some hands-on features inside Claude itself may need a paid Claude plan, so check before you rely on them. The free courses and the practice exercises can be done on whatever access your team already provides.

Which courses give a certificate?

Claude 101 and AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations state a certificate of completion on their course pages. For the others the page does not say, so we do not promise one.

Is this a technical course?

Steps 1 to 4 are written for everyone. The Claude Code and MCP courses are for developers, so we ask you only to read along for the ideas.

What should I never paste into Claude?

Passwords, keys, personal data about customers or colleagues, and anything your team has marked confidential. Remove names and details first, and follow your team's AI-use guidance.

Can I trust what Claude writes?

Treat it as a fast, capable first draft from a colleague who sometimes makes things up. You stay responsible for the result, so check facts, names and dates before you share it.

Track your progress

Log each step as a to-do in your GitLab workspace: one issue per step, with the label learning::pilot. Add a note with what you learned and a link or screenshot of your completion, then close the issue when you finish the step.
