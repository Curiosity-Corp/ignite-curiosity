Iteration done right: Agile & Scrum for Product Managers

Every product team you will ever join says it is "agile." A PM who really understands sprints, flow and honest estimation can tell the difference between a team that is learning fast and one that is just holding more meetings. In 2026 there is a new twist: AI agents now pick up tickets, open drafts and ask for review, right alongside people. The PM who can set clear goals, limit work in progress and define "done" for both humans and agents is the one who keeps the whole team moving. This course is about delivery. If you are looking for Agile as a way of learning, see our related reading, Agile Learning.

What you'll be able to do

Run Scrum end to end

Explain the three accountabilities, five events and three artifacts of Scrum, and facilitate sprint planning with a clear, testable sprint goal.

Manage flow with Kanban

Set work-in-progress limits and read flow metrics such as cycle time and throughput, so you can spot a stuck board before the deadline does.

Refine and estimate honestly

Keep a backlog healthy through regular refinement, write small user stories, and size them with story points without turning estimates into promises.

Read the charts

Use velocity, burndown and cycle time to start good conversations, and run retrospectives that end with one change the team actually tries.

Understand scaling basics

Describe, in plain English, how several teams share a roadmap without a giant process, and when a framework is worth the overhead.

Lead a human + agent team

Assign work to AI agents, write a definition of done for agent output, and keep a person accountable for every review.

Practice in GitLab

Do these on your real project board in your own GitLab workspace. No code and no new projects needed. Our GitLab is Ultimate, so iterations, board WIP limits and scoped labels are all available to you. If a menu is missing, it is probably hidden by your role, so ask your coach.

1. Run one real iteration

Open Plan › Iterations in your group, create an iteration cadence of one or two weeks, and pull 5 to 8 issues into the current iteration. Write the sprint goal in one sentence at the top of the iteration description.

2. Build a sprint board with workflow labels

Create scoped labels workflow::ready, workflow::in progress, workflow::in review and workflow::done. Because they share the workflow:: prefix, an issue can only hold one at a time. Then make an issue board with one list per label, filtered to the current iteration.

3. Set WIP limits and estimate with weights

On the in progress list, set a work-in-progress limit (try 3 for a team of 2). Then give each sprint issue an issue weight using story points (1, 2, 3, 5, 8). Anything over 8 gets split into smaller issues. Agree as a team what happens when the limit is hit: finish something before you start something new.

4. Watch the burndown, then run a retro

Midway through the iteration, open the iteration page and read its burndown chart. Is the line flat? That is a conversation, not a verdict. On the last day, create a "Retro" issue with three headings (What went well, What was hard, One thing we will change) and a checklist. Next sprint, copy it with the /clone quick action instead of starting over. A maintainer can later turn it into an official issue template.

The agentic angle

Agents do not change what Scrum is for. They change who can hold a ticket. In a healthy agentic sprint, an AI agent is a team member with a clear job, a visible card on the board and a human who answers for the result.

  • Agent-owned issues: mark them with a label such as owner::agent and name the human reviewer in the description. Small, well-bounded issues (weight 1 to 3) suit agents best.
  • A definition of done for agent work: the acceptance criteria are written, the output is linked in the issue, sources or assumptions are listed, and nothing moves to workflow::done until a person has reviewed it.
  • Human review is a WIP limit too: an agent can produce drafts faster than people can check them, so cap the in review list.
  • Measure it like any other work: compare cycle time and rework for agent-owned and human-owned issues at the retro.

Exercise: sprint planning with an AI teammate

Paste your sprint goal and backlog titles into Claude (or another assistant) and ask it to: (1) flag issues that are too big and propose splits, (2) suggest story-point weights with its reasoning, and (3) draft acceptance criteria for two issues. Then decide for yourself: keep, change or reject each suggestion and write down why. Finish by writing a 4-line "definition of done for agent work" and paste it at the top of your board's description. Never paste confidential information into a public AI tool.

Agile & Scrum FAQ

Is the whole path really free?

Yes. The guides, the IBM SkillsBuild badge, the Microsoft Learn module and the Scrum.org Open Assessments all cost $0. Only the optional PSM I exam is paid ($200 USD), and you never need it to finish this course.

Do I need a Scrum Master certificate to get a PM job?

Not usually. Many teams care more about whether you can run a good sprint. Real practice on your GitLab board, plus a free badge, is a strong start. Add PSM I later if an employer asks for it.

Scrum or Kanban, which should I use?

Scrum gives a steady rhythm of sprints and goals. Kanban suits a steady stream of requests. Many teams blend them, which is why you practice both: sprints for planning, WIP limits for flow.

Are story points hours?

No. Story points compare relative size and uncertainty, not time. Your team's velocity turns them into a forecast over several sprints.

Can an AI agent be on my sprint team?

It can own tasks, but a person stays accountable. Give agent work a clear definition of done and a named human reviewer, as in the agentic section above.

Track your progress

Log each step as a to-do in your GitLab workspace: one issue per step, labeled learning::pilot. Close the issue when you finish the step, and paste your badge link in the last one.
