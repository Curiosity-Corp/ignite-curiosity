See the Product through Your Customer’s Eyes

Customers do not experience your roadmap. They experience a string of moments: an ad, a sign-up, a first screen, a support reply. A PM who can see those moments, and prove which ones hurt, decides better than one who only reads feature requests. In 2026 AI agents can summarise research in minutes, which makes human judgement (asking good questions, spotting bias, talking to real people) the skill that matters most. This course is part of the PILOT Program “Launch & experience” pillar. For the research and data-science side, see UX Research & Data Science.

What you’ll be able to do

Run a real user interview

Plan and run a short, non-leading interview, then capture what people actually did and why, using Jobs-to-be-Done thinking instead of asking what they want.

Map a customer journey

Turn research into a journey map with stages, goals, emotions and pain points, and spot the moments that matter most.

Inventory every touchpoint

List each place a customer meets your product or team (emails, app screens, support, docs) and see which ones are weak or missing.

Test usability and accessibility

Check a flow against Nielsen’s 10 heuristics, run a five-person usability test, and apply accessibility basics.

Improve onboarding and activation

Define the first “aha” moment, find where new users drop off, and write a testable improvement.

Measure the experience

Choose between CSAT, NPS, CES and task success, and say what each one can and cannot tell you.

Practice in GitLab

Do these on your real GitLab project board. No code, and no new projects.

Exercise 1: Build a journey-stage label set

  1. In your GitLab workspace, open Manage › Labels and create these scoped labels: journey::awareness, journey::consideration, journey::decision, journey::onboarding, journey::adoption, journey::advocacy.
  2. Scoped labels (the double colon) let an issue carry only one journey stage at a time.
  3. Give each label a description in plain English, for example “The customer is trying the product for the first time.”

Exercise 2: Create a touchpoint inventory issue

  1. Create one issue titled Touchpoint inventory: [your product].
  2. In the description, add a table with these columns: touchpoint, journey stage, owner, channel, what the customer is trying to do, known pain.
  3. Fill in at least ten touchpoints, such as the ad, the sign-up page, the welcome email, the first screen, a support reply, and the invoice.
  4. Give the issue the label journey::awareness or the stage you know least about, and ask a teammate to add what you missed.

Exercise 3: Make a reusable usability-test issue

  1. Write one issue called TEMPLATE: Usability test with sections for goal, task scenarios (3 to 5), participants (5), success criteria, observation notes, and findings.
  2. Each time you plan a test, make a copy with the /clone quick action. File-based issue templates live in the repository, so ask your coach whether a maintainer can add yours as an official template.
  3. Run a test with a real person on one task and record notes in the copy. Label it with the journey stage it covers.

Exercise 4: Link bugs and requests to journey stages

  1. Pick five open bugs or feature requests. Give each exactly one journey:: label.
  2. In the touchpoint inventory issue, add each one with the /relate #issue quick action so the customer pain and the work item are connected.
  3. Write one sentence in each linked issue: which customer moment does this affect, and how would we know it improved?

Exercise 5: Set up a board by journey stage

  1. Open Plan › Issue boards and create a new board called Customer journey.
  2. Add one list per journey:: label, in order from awareness to advocacy.
  3. Look for empty or overloaded columns. A column with many bugs and no ideas is a conversation to start with your team.

The Agentic Angle

Claude is very good at the slow part of research: reading ten pages of interview notes and proposing themes. It is not good at knowing whether those themes are true. Your job moves from typing summaries to challenging them and checking them with real people.

Practical exercise: from interview notes to a draft journey map

  1. Run three to five short interviews. Remove names, emails and anything identifying before you paste any notes into Claude. Never share confidential customer data.
  2. Ask Claude: “Group these notes into themes. For each theme give the participants who said it and a direct quote. Mark any theme supported by fewer than three people as weak.”
  3. Bias check: ask “Which of my questions were leading? What evidence in these notes contradicts each theme? What groups of users are missing?” Read the answers critically and check the quotes against your original notes, because AI can invent or blur quotes.
  4. Ask Claude to draft a journey map as a table: stage, customer goal, touchpoint, emotion, pain, opportunity. Paste it into your touchpoint inventory issue as a draft.
  5. Validate with two real users: walk them through the map and ask “what is wrong or missing?” Update the issue, and note what you changed and why.

UX & Journeys FAQ

Do I need design skills?

No. A PM’s job here is to understand people, ask good questions, and connect what users struggle with to the work the team plans. You do not need to draw or code.

Is this really free?

Yes for the recommended path: free badges, free courses and free audits. The Google certificate is free only if Coursera approves financial aid, and the Alison and edX certificates are optional paid add-ons. You never need to pay to finish this course.

How is this different from the UX Research & Data Science page?

That page covers research and data careers. This course is for PMs: connecting research to journeys, touchpoints, labels and boards. See UX Research & Data Science for the deeper research track.

Can I talk to real users if I am a junior PM?

Yes. Start with two or three teammates, friends who match your user, or customers your team already speaks to. Ask a coach before contacting customers directly.

What about AI-generated personas?

Treat them as a starting guess to test, never as evidence. Only real people can confirm a need, so the agentic exercise below always ends with real-user validation.
