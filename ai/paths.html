Free AI learning, matched to you

In 2026 AI is already part of homework, hiring, health apps and the news, and by 2040 it will be as ordinary as the internet. The people who thrive will not be the ones who memorize tools. They will be the curious ones who can ask good questions, check the answers and decide what is right. The world’s best AI academies now teach for free. This page picks the best steps from them and lines them up by who you are, so you can start today and never pay a cent. If you coach others, the Coaching Kit below gives you a four-week circle, discussion prompts and safety tips. Looking for the full catalogue of providers? Visit the AI Academy Atlas.

What you’ll be able to do

Choose your own road

Pick the path that fits you or your learners, from a family evening to a developer portfolio, with every step free to start.

Earn proof you can show

Collect free certificates and badges from Anthropic, Elements of AI, Kaggle, Hugging Face and more, with honest labels on what is free and what is not.

Use AI with good judgment

Prompt clearly, check the answer, spot bias and know when a person must decide.

Coach a curiosity circle

Run a four-week AI circle with free tools, discussion prompts, a prompt exercise and a myth-vs-fact game.

Keep young learners safe

Know the official minimum ages of the big tools and the habits that protect minors.

Stay current

Follow a short list of free newsletters, podcasts and official blogs so the learning does not stop.

Coaching Kit

Everything a Curiosity Coach needs to turn the paths above into a living, human learning experience. Nothing here needs a paid tool.

Run a 4-week AI curiosity circle

Six to ten learners, 60 minutes a week. The coach facilitates; the learners do the thinking. For learners under 18, the coach holds the account and drives any chatbot on screen.

Week 1 · Notice AI

Warm-up: where did you meet AI this week? Then train a model together with Teachable Machine and play Quick, Draw!. Ask: how did the computer learn, and what did it get wrong?

Week 2 · Prompt and check

Run the prompt-practice exercise below on one shared screen. Compare three versions of a prompt, then fact-check the answer against a trusted source.

Week 3 · Fair and safe

Do AI for Oceans to see how training data shapes results, then use two or three of the discussion prompts below. Finish with the myth-vs-fact game.

Week 4 · Make and showcase

Each learner shares one thing: a small project, a before-and-after prompt, or a question they still have. Plan their next free course from the paths above and show them how to display a badge.

Discussion prompts: ethics, bias and safety

  • An AI learned from photos that mostly showed one kind of person. Who might it treat unfairly? How could we find out?
  • If an AI gives a wrong answer with total confidence, who is responsible for the mistake: the maker, the user, or both?
  • What should a person always decide, even when an AI is available to help (a grade, a medical choice, a hiring decision)? Why?
  • Is it fair to use AI for homework? Where is the line between help and cheating in your class or workplace?
  • What personal information would you never type into a chatbot? What could happen to it?
  • How can you tell whether an image, video or voice is real? What would you do if someone shared a fake of you?
  • Who is missing from the data an AI learned from, and who benefits when AI works well?
  • When is it kind and honest to say “an AI helped me with this”?

Deeper reading for coaches: the UNESCO ethics recommendation, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the UNICEF policy guidance on AI for children.

Prompt-practice exercise

Role, Task, Context, Format, Check (our five-part prompt)

Pick a real task, such as planning a family picnic or explaining photosynthesis to a ten-year-old. Work in four rounds and write down what changes.

  1. Vague prompt: type only the topic. Read the answer together.
  2. Add Role and Task: “You are a friendly science tutor. Explain…”
  3. Add Context and Format: who it is for, how long, a table or a list.
  4. Add Check: ask it to list what it is unsure about, then verify two facts yourself with a trusted source.

Debrief: which round helped most, and what did the AI still get wrong? Never paste private or confidential information. For a guided version of the same idea, see the 4D framework (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence) in Anthropic’s AI Fluency course.

AI myth vs fact

Read each myth aloud. Learners vote “myth” or “fact,” then the coach reveals the answer and the group explains why.

Myth: AI understands things like a person.

Fact: Today’s AI finds patterns in data and predicts likely outputs. It can sound human without having human understanding or experience.

Myth: If AI says it, it must be true.

Fact: AI can state wrong things confidently. Always check important facts with a trusted source.

Myth: AI is neutral, so it cannot be biased.

Fact: AI learns from human-made data and design choices, so it can repeat or amplify unfairness.

Myth: You need to be a coder to learn AI.

Fact: Many free courses above need no code at all. Coding helps for building, not for understanding or using AI well.

Fact: How you ask changes what you get.

Clear context, a goal and a format usually give better answers. That is why prompt practice is a real skill.

Fact: People stay accountable for AI-assisted work.

A tool can help draft, but a person owns the decision and the result.

Showcase your badges

  • Collect proof as you go: save each certificate PDF or badge link in one folder named for the course.
  • Credly badges (for example IBM SkillsBuild): accept the badge in your Credly account, then use its share button to post it to LinkedIn or copy the public link.
  • On LinkedIn: add each credential under the “Licenses & certifications” section of your profile and paste the credential URL. Add the skill it taught to your Skills list.
  • Say what you did: one sentence per badge, such as “Completed Anthropic’s AI Fluency course and applied it to plan a community workshop.”
  • Under 18? Social networks have their own minimum ages, so ask a parent or guardian and check the network’s rules. A printed certificate or a simple portfolio page the family controls works well too.
  • Be honest about the label: a certificate of completion is not an exam credential. Say which one you earned.

Safety tips for minors using AI tools

Minimum ages are set by each company and change over time, so re-check the official page before every new term. We list what each official page says today.

ToolOfficial minimum ageSource
Claude (Anthropic)18 or older to create and use an account.Claude Help Center
ChatGPT (OpenAI)Not meant for children under 13; ages 13 to 18 need parental consent. (OpenAI’s help page blocks automated checks, so we confirmed this through search of the help center.)OpenAI Help Center
Gemini (Google)13 (or the applicable age in your country) or over for a personal or school Google Account. A Family Link account for a child under 13 cannot use the Gemini web app. Work accounts must be 18 or over.Gemini Apps Help
Microsoft CopilotAt least 13, and higher in some countries depending on local rules. Microsoft notes a newer Copilot app may differ, so read its current article.Microsoft Support
Khanmigo (Khan Academy)18 or older to sign up directly; under 18 needs a parent or guardian to add them. Learner access is paid; teacher tools are free.Khanmigo
  • Coach-led for the youngest: if a tool’s minimum age is not met, an adult runs the screen and the child only watches or suggests ideas. Use the free browser tools in Week 1 (Teachable Machine, Quick, Draw!) and Code.org lessons, which are built for classrooms.
  • No personal details: no full names, addresses, school names, photos of faces, passwords or private family information in any prompt.
  • Check everything: teach the habit “AI said it, now I verify it.” Do not rely on a chatbot for health, safety or emotional crises. Talk to a trusted adult.
  • Chatbots are not friends: keep a human in every important conversation, and tell a trusted adult if something an AI says feels strange or upsetting.
  • Deepfakes and kindness: never make or share a fake image, voice or video of a real person. Talk about what to do if one appears.
  • Make a family or circle agreement: which tools, when, and who sits nearby. Review it each term.
  • More guidance: Common Sense Media on AI, UNICEF policy guidance on AI for children and National Cybersecurity Alliance.

Learning Paths FAQ

Is every path really free?

Every step in the seven paths is free to start. Where a certificate costs money (DeepLearning.AI, CS50 on edX, Google AI Essentials) we say so on the card, and those paid items sit in the “Later, when sponsored” block or are marked free to audit.

Which path should I start with?

Not sure? Take path (a). It needs no coding and gives you a certificate in the first week. Then choose the path for your role.

Why do some cards say “certificate not confirmed”?

We only promise what an official page states. If a provider does not clearly say a certificate or badge exists, we say so rather than guess.

Are the hours exact?

No. They are our estimates unless the provider lists hours. Plan for more time if you are new to the topic.

Can my child use ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini?

It depends on age and the tool. See the minimum-age table in the Coaching Kit. For younger learners, a coach or parent should lead and use classroom-built tools such as Code.org lessons.

How do I show off a badge?

Use Credly or the provider’s share button, add the credential on LinkedIn if you are of age, and keep a folder of proofs. See “Showcase your badges.”
