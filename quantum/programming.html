Build your first quantum circuit this week

You do not need a physics degree or a quantum computer to start programming one. In 2026 the major toolkits are free and open source, several companies let you run small circuits on real hardware at no cost, and a browser is enough to begin. The skill that grows from here is simple to name: describe a problem as a circuit, run it, read noisy results honestly, and improve it. That habit points toward 2040, when error-corrected machines may need people who think in both classical and quantum code. Today's devices are still small and noisy, so we teach this as exploration and craft, not as a promise of instant speedups. This page is the B in QUBIT Quest: Building circuits.

What you'll be able to do

Build and draw a circuit

Create a Bell-state circuit in code, draw it as a diagram and explain what each gate does to the qubits.

Run it and read the results

Simulate a circuit thousands of times, plot the counts and explain why the answers are a spread of outcomes, not one number.

Try real hardware

Send a tiny job to a real quantum processor and compare its noisy results with a perfect simulator.

Choose a toolkit sensibly

Compare Qiskit, Cirq, Q#, PennyLane, CUDA-Q, pytket and others by language, goal and cost, then pick one for your next project.

Work without installing anything

Use a browser composer, a hosted notebook or Google Colab to code quantum programs on a school Chromebook.

Connect circuits to AI

Wrap a small circuit in PennyLane and train it like a model, as a first step toward quantum machine learning (see our algorithms page).

Your free learning path

Pick one toolkit first and go deep, then sample a second. Every step below is free. Hours are our own estimates. Where a certificate is not confirmed on the official page, we say so.

Step 1 · IBM Qiskit and the IBM Quantum Platform

Provider: IBM. You earn: working Qiskit skills. The IBM Quantum Learning course Basics of quantum information is the first in a four-course series, and IBM offers an optional badge exam at IBM Training; the exam price is not confirmed on the course pages, so check before you book it. Free plan: the Open Plan lists up to 10 minutes of real quantum computer (QPU) time. IBM's docs describe this as per 28-day rolling window; the pricing page words it as per month. Active users may be eligible to request more time. Cost: Free course · About 10 hours (estimate) · Level: Beginner

Open the courseSee the free plan

Step 2 · Google Cirq tutorials

Provider: Google Quantum AI. You earn: Cirq skills (no certificate). Start with Cirq basics, which builds and simulates your first circuit. Cirq is free and open source. Note that running on Google's own processors is restricted to an approved group, so treat Cirq as a simulator and learning tool for now. Cost: Free course · About 4 hours (estimate) · Level: Intermediate (assumes some quantum background)

Cirq basicsMore tutorials

Step 3 · Microsoft Quantum Development Kit (Q#)

Provider: Microsoft. You earn: Q# skills (certificate not confirmed). The QDK is free and open source, and you do not need an Azure account to use it. Follow the Q# quickstart, then the Grover search tutorial. Azure Quantum itself needs an Azure account and workspace for real hardware, and we could not confirm a current free-credits offer, so check the Azure Quantum pricing page before relying on one. Cost: Free course (QDK and docs) · About 5 hours (estimate) · Level: Intermediate

Q# quickstartAzure Quantum pricing

Step 4 · Xanadu PennyLane Codebook and demos

Provider: Xanadu. You earn: a strong foundation in quantum computing and QML through 15 listed Codebook modules, from single-qubit gates to Shor's algorithm and Hamiltonian simulation (certificate not confirmed). PennyLane runs on many simulators and hardware backends. Cost: Free course · About 20 hours (estimate) · Level: Beginner to Intermediate

Step 5 · NVIDIA CUDA-Q Academic

Provider: NVIDIA. You earn: hands-on skill in hybrid quantum-classical code (certificate not confirmed). CUDA-Q Academic is an open curriculum of Jupyter notebooks with a Quick Start to Quantum for beginners and advanced paths up to error correction. Notebooks can run locally or on qBraid, CoCalc or Google Colab. Cost: Free course · About 8 hours (estimate) · Level: Beginner to Advanced

Open CUDA-Q AcademicAbout CUDA-Q

Step 6 · Quantinuum pytket

Provider: Quantinuum. You earn: skill at compiling the same circuit for different devices (no certificate). pytket installs from PyPI with pip, and the docs include a user guide and getting started page. Access to Quantinuum hardware is a commercial offering; we could not confirm a free hardware tier, so use the open-source toolkit with simulators. Cost: Free course (open-source docs) · About 4 hours (estimate) · Level: Intermediate

Step 7 · Classiq (optional)

Provider: Classiq. You earn: a taste of high-level quantum design (certificate not confirmed). The open classiq-library on GitHub and the docs are free to read. We could not verify a free tier for the Classiq platform itself: its academia page lists education programs but states no price, so ask Classiq or your school before counting on free access. Cost: Free course (docs and library only; platform pricing not confirmed) · About 3 hours (estimate) · Level: Intermediate

Later, when sponsored · Amazon Braket

Provider: Amazon Web Services. Braket is a paid cloud service for running circuits on several vendors' hardware. AWS lists one hour of included on-demand simulation per month for the first twelve months of using Braket, and a free local simulator in the Braket SDK. Hardware tasks are billed per use, so check the official pricing page for real prices before any session. For learners, the free local simulator and the example notebooks are the right place to begin. Cost: Free local simulator; cloud use is paid · About 4 hours (estimate) · Level: Intermediate

Hands-on: install-free labs

These run in a browser, so they work on a school laptop. Most need a free account.

IBM Quantum Composer

Drag gates onto qubit lines, watch probabilities and the Q-sphere update, then switch to the code editor, which supports OpenQASM 2 and 3. Sign in to save your work. Try it first: make a Bell state and predict the outcomes before you press run.

Open Composer

qBraid Lab (free plan)

A web-based IDE for quantum computing with ready-made environments for machine learning, chemistry and error correction. qBraid documents a free plan with a monthly CPU-hour allowance; its pricing page and docs list slightly different limits, so read the current numbers when you sign up. Free here means free of qBraid credits: jobs on some partner devices bill to a separate account.

Check free limitsqBraid

Google Colab notebooks

Colab gives you a free hosted notebook in the browser, with nothing to install except the toolkit with one pip command. Google publishes a Cirq getting-started notebook that opens in Colab, and CUDA-Q Academic lists Colab as a way to run its lessons.

Cirq notebook in ColabGoogle Colab

PennyLane demos and the QDK playground

PennyLane demonstrations are runnable tutorials, from a first variational circuit to quantum chemistry. Microsoft's QDK also links a browser playground that opens in vscode.dev. Both let you read, change and rerun real code in minutes.

For Curiosity Coaches

Coaches do not need to be quantum experts. Your job is to keep learners curious, help them run the code and ask good questions.

Discussion prompts
  • Why does the same circuit give different counts each run? What does that tell us about measurement?
  • How is a quantum program like a recipe, and how is it not?
  • The real machine gave "wrong" answers the simulator did not. What might cause that?
  • If you could choose only one toolkit for a school club, which would you pick and why?
4-week plan (about 1 hour per week)
  • Week 1: Composer. Build and explain a Bell state without code.
  • Week 2: Recreate it in Qiskit or PennyLane in a notebook and plot the counts.
  • Week 3: Run on a real device if the learner has a free IBM account, then compare with the simulator.
  • Week 4: Try a second toolkit and write three sentences on which felt better.

Age and level fit: Composer and the Week 1 activity suit curious teens (about 13+) with no coding. Python notebooks suit learners comfortable with basic Python, such as high school and above. Cirq, pytket and Q# are better for intermediate learners and career changers. Always check each provider's age rules before a learner makes an account.

Quantum Programming FAQ

Do I need a quantum computer?

No. Every toolkit here runs a simulator on an ordinary laptop or in the browser. Real hardware is a bonus, not a requirement.

How much real hardware time is free?

IBM's Open Plan lists up to 10 minutes of QPU time. That is enough for many small experiments, because each job often runs for seconds. IBM's docs describe it per 28-day rolling window while its pricing page says per month. Check IBM's plans overview for the current rule.

Which toolkit should a beginner pick?

Qiskit has the most tutorials and a free real-hardware plan. PennyLane is a gentle choice if you already like machine learning. You can learn the ideas in one and carry them to another.

Is Amazon Braket free?

No, it is a paid cloud service. The Braket SDK includes a free local simulator, and AWS lists one hour of included on-demand simulation per month for the first twelve months. Hardware use is billed.

Can I learn without knowing Python?

Yes, to start. IBM Quantum Composer lets you build circuits by dragging gates. Add Python later; basic loops and functions are enough for most first programs.

Will this get me a job?

It builds real skills and a portfolio of small projects, which is what quantum teams look at. Treat it as a strong start, then follow the Transition steps in QUBIT Quest.

Honest note

Free allowances change. We checked the official pages in October 2026, but always read the current terms before you plan a class around a quota.
