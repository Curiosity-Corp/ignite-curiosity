Algorithms that show what quantum computers are really for

An algorithm is the reason a quantum computer is worth building. A few have rigorous proofs of speedups, many are promising ideas still being tested, and some may never beat a good classical method. We want you to learn all three kinds, and to be able to say which is which. Today's machines are in what John Preskill named the NISQ era: Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum devices, with too few qubits and too much noise for the famous algorithms at useful size. The long-term hope, which our 2040 framing points toward, is fault tolerance: error-corrected machines. Google reported in Nature that its error-corrected memory improved as the code grew, a milestone for that road. Real progress is happening, and so are real limits. This page is the I in QUBIT Quest: Industry algorithms.

Read next: Preskill, "Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond" and Google Quantum AI, error correction below the surface code threshold (Nature).

What you'll be able to do

Explain the classic algorithms

Describe what Deutsch-Jozsa, Bernstein-Vazirani, Grover, QFT, phase estimation and Shor do, and what kind of speedup each one really gives.

Explain Shor for security

Say in plain words why a large fault-tolerant quantum computer would threaten RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, and why NIST is already moving to new standards.

Understand VQE and QAOA

Describe how hybrid quantum-classical loops work, why they suit noisy hardware and why they come with no guaranteed advantage.

Grasp error correction basics

Explain why qubits need many physical qubits per logical qubit, what a code threshold means and why this decides when big algorithms become possible.

Run a small QML model

Train a tiny variational quantum classifier in PennyLane or Qiskit Machine Learning and say honestly what it did and did not show.

Separate hype from progress

Read an industry claim, find the problem size, the baseline and the hardware used, and ask whether it is a demonstration or a business result.

Why Shor matters for cryptography

Much of today's public-key security, including RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, relies on problems that Shor's algorithm would solve efficiently on a large fault-tolerant quantum computer. Nobody has built one. Researchers keep lowering the estimated cost: a 2025 paper from Google estimates that RSA-2048 could be factored with fewer than a million noisy qubits, still far beyond today's machines. Because stolen data can be stored and decrypted later, governments are moving early. NIST has published the first post-quantum standards, including FIPS 203, 204 and 205, and its draft IR 8547 lays out a timeline for retiring quantum-vulnerable algorithms (the draft points to deprecation by 2030 and disallowance by 2035). The "by 2040" idea is our own forward-looking framing of where those timelines point, not a NIST requirement. Our SHIELD 2040 area follows this story from the security side.

  • Gidney (2025): How to factor 2048 bit RSA integers with less than a million noisy qubits
  • NIST FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204, FIPS 205
  • NIST IR 8547 (draft): Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards

Quantum machine learning (QML), carefully

QML asks whether quantum circuits can learn from data in useful ways. It is a lively research area and a great way to learn, but there is no proven practical advantage today. Loading classical data into a quantum state is costly, and good classical methods are hard to beat. Treat QML demos as a way to understand variational circuits, not as evidence that quantum AI is here.

Industry use cases, without the hype

These are the areas where quantum computing is being studied. For each one, ask: what is the problem size, what is the best classical baseline, and which hardware was used?

Chemistry

Simulating molecules is a natural fit, since molecules are quantum systems. Today, VQE-style experiments handle only small molecules that classical computers also solve. Large, useful chemistry is expected to need fault tolerance.

Optimisation

Logistics, scheduling and routing are often named. QAOA and related heuristics are being tested, but advantage over strong classical solvers has not been shown. Grover gives at most a quadratic speedup for search.

Finance

Risk analysis, option pricing and portfolio optimisation are active research topics. Many proposed speedups rely on large fault-tolerant machines, and the cost of loading data is a known hurdle.

Materials

Batteries, catalysts and superconductors rely on quantum effects that are hard to simulate classically. This is regarded as a likely early beneficiary, once error-corrected hardware exists.

Watch the evidence: IBM quantum advantage tracker (an open community effort that compares advantage candidates with the best classical methods), and IBM: Quantum business foundations for a business view.

For Curiosity Coaches

Discussion prompts
  • Grover gives a quadratic speedup, not a magic one. When is "a bit faster" worth a very expensive machine?
  • If Shor's algorithm will need a machine that does not exist yet, why is NIST acting now?
  • VQE has no proven advantage. What would convince you that it does?
  • Read one company announcement: what was the problem size, and what was the classical baseline?
4-week plan (about 1 to 2 hours per week)
  • Week 1: Grover and the idea of quadratic speedup, with the PennyLane Grover demo.
  • Week 2: QFT, phase estimation and Shor: read the cryptography section and the NIST links.
  • Week 3: VQE or QAOA demo, then discuss why noise matters.
  • Week 4: One QML demo plus a "hype or progress?" debate on a real announcement.

Algorithms FAQ

Can quantum computers break encryption today?

No. Breaking RSA or elliptic-curve cryptography with Shor's algorithm would need a large fault-tolerant machine, which does not exist yet. NIST has already published post-quantum standards, including FIPS 203, 204 and 205, so systems can prepare early.

What is the NISQ era?

A term from John Preskill for today's Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum devices: real, programmable, but too noisy and small for the famous algorithms at useful scale.

Is quantum machine learning better than normal machine learning?

Not that anyone has shown for practical tasks. It is a research area and a good way to learn variational circuits. Be wary of claims of quantum AI advantage without a strong classical baseline.

Do I need heavy math?

It helps to know vectors and matrices. IBM's courses go deeper into linear algebra, while PennyLane Codebook and demos let you explore with code first.

Which algorithm should I learn first?

Grover. It is easy to state, has a proven speedup and teaches amplitude amplification. Then do QFT and phase estimation, which lead to Shor.

Are the badges free?

The lessons are free. IBM's badge exams are taken at IBM Training, and we could not confirm the exam price, so check before you plan on a badge.

Think like a skeptic

For every claim, ask: what problem, what size, compared with what, on which hardware? It is the best quantum skill a learner can build.
