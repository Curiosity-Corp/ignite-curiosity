Start here: Cyber Security Foundations

In 2026 almost every job touches data, and the people who understand how to protect it are in demand. The path to 2040 starts with a handful of ideas: the CIA triad, threats and risk, how networks and operating systems work, a little cryptography and good everyday habits. NIST's NICE Framework describes the work roles you can grow into, and its Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 shows how organizations think about risk. This page is pillar S of SHIELD 2040, Security foundations. It gives you a free, ordered path from curious beginner to ready for an entry-level job.

What you'll be able to do

Explain the CIA triad

Describe confidentiality, integrity and availability with everyday examples, and spot which one an incident hurts.

Tell threats, vulnerabilities and risk apart

Use the plain-language chain: a threat exploits a vulnerability, and risk is how likely and how costly that is.

Read a network and an OS

Describe IP addresses, ports, DNS, firewalls and how a computer manages users, files and permissions.

Understand cryptography basics

Explain hashing, symmetric and public-key encryption and why strong passwords and multi-factor sign-in matter.

Practice security awareness

Recognize phishing and social engineering, and teach family and friends how to stay safe online.

Choose a first role

Map your interests to a NICE Framework work role so you know which entry-level job you are heading toward.

Core concepts in five ideas

Every credential below teaches some version of these five ideas. Learn them first and the courses will feel familiar rather than overwhelming.

1. The CIA triad

Confidentiality (only the right people see it), Integrity (it is accurate and unaltered) and Availability (it works when needed). Almost every security decision protects one of these three.

2. Threats, vulnerabilities and risk

A threat is something that could cause harm (a criminal, a mistake, a storm). A vulnerability is a weakness (an unpatched app, a reused password). Risk combines how likely the harm is with how bad it would be. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 turns this thinking into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.

Read NIST CSF 2.0

3. Networking and operating-system basics

Know how data travels (IP, DNS, ports, TCP/UDP) and how Windows and Linux handle accounts, permissions and logs. Defenders read these things all day, which is why the Cisco Networking Basics step below comes early.

4. Cryptography basics

Hashing proves a file has not changed, encryption hides it, and digital signatures prove who sent it. Public-key methods used today are being replaced by post-quantum standards over the coming years. You will explore that in the Emerging threats part of SHIELD 2040.

Explore SHIELD 2040

5. Security awareness

Most real attacks begin with a person, not a machine. Learn to pause before clicking, use a password manager and turn on multi-factor sign-in. The CISA "Secure Our World" campaign gives four simple habits you can teach anyone.

For Curiosity Coaches

Use this page as a six-week launch for a learner who is new to cybersecurity. Keep each session to 45 to 60 minutes and end every one with a "what would an attacker try?" question.

Discussion prompts
  • Pick one app you use daily. Which part of the CIA triad matters most for it, and why?
  • What is one threat, one vulnerability and one risk in your own home network?
  • If a password leaks, what protects you next? Why is multi-factor sign-in a second layer?
  • Look at the NICE work roles. Which one sounds like you on your best day?
A 4-week starter plan
  • Week 1: the five core ideas and Step 1. Learner sets up a password manager and multi-factor sign-in.
  • Week 2: Step 2, Cisco Introduction to Cybersecurity. Discuss one real breach in the news.
  • Week 3: Step 3, Networking Basics (start). Draw the home network on paper.
  • Week 4: Step 4, the IBM SkillsBuild badge assessment, or Fortinet NSE 1. Then pick a work role from the NICE catalog and plan the next four weeks. Move on to Hands-on Defense.
Age and level fit
  • Ages 10 to 12: do Step 1 together, then use the discussion prompts. Skip the sign-ups.
  • Ages 13 to 17: Steps 1 to 3 with a coach or teacher creating the accounts. IBM badge eligibility for under-18s is not confirmed, so check first.
  • Adults and career-changers: the full path, then CISA Learning for role-specific depth.

Foundations FAQ

Is the whole path really free?

Steps 1 to 7 and the optional SANS items cost $0. The paid items (ISC2 CC exam, Microsoft SC-900 exam, the Google certificate) are only in the "Later, when sponsored" block, and you never need them to finish.

Is the free ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity offer still open?

No. ISC2 closed new enrollments in its One Million Certified in Cybersecurity program on May 20, 2026. The exam is now $199 USD with a $50 USD annual maintenance fee, so we list it as a later, sponsored goal.

Do I need a certificate to get an entry-level job?

Often not on day one, but a recognized credential and hands-on practice together help a lot. Pair a badge from this page with the labs on the Hands-on Defense page.

What is the NICE Framework?

It is NIST's common language for cybersecurity work: work roles, competency areas, and task, knowledge and skill statements. Use it to pick a target job and see what to learn next.

Is there an age limit?

It depends on the provider. IBM's badge page says adults 18+. Most other pages do not state a minimum age, so under-18 learners should sign up with a coach, parent or teacher.
